## Nature of the request

## Humanities content

The project would examine three related ideas. First, government records describe people through administrative purposes: a land patent records a transfer of specified tracts, while a census reports household and property information. The corrected 1860 Mulberry Township image lists Spencer Horner and Permelia; the 1857 patent lists four tracts. Neither explains the later attackers’ motives. Displaying record and inference side by side would help visitors practice close reading rather than mistake property valuations for evidence of intent.

Second, memories of violence develop through family transmission and public commemoration. A marker reading gives William Riley Horner’s death as 10 August 1864 and attributes responsibility to parties known to his family. A later account attributed to 1942 describes an attack and reprisals. Visitors would ask what each source preserves, when it was made, and which questions remain beyond it. The project would neither present a reprisal count as a proved casualty total nor infer official refusal to investigate from missing records.

Third, uncertainty can be made legible without abandoning historical interpretation. The archive’s prior census misidentification and reversed patent aliquot demonstrate why provenance and correction matter. A label could show a claim being narrowed as a source is checked. The exhibition would connect this local example to the broader history of irregular warfare and civilian protection. Proposed contextual reading includes Daniel E. Sutherland’s *A Savage Conflict* (2009) and Thomas A. DeBlack’s *With Fire and Sword* (2003). Publisher listings were checked, but full texts and relevant page-level arguments have not been integrated in this draft. ## Project formats

The envisioned exhibition would have four compact sections: household and land; wartime loss; family memory and memorials; and the historian’s evidence desk. A census facsimile with a readable partial transcription would introduce administrative evidence. A patent detail would let visitors compare the four tract descriptions without suggesting a verified modern house site. A short, attributed family-account paraphrase and a permitted marker image would invite comparison of document dates, event dates and memorial dates. The evidence desk would ask visitors to choose a next record and explain what it could resolve.

Large-print labels, image descriptions, seated reading access and a printable text companion would be tested during planning. No graphic injury detail is necessary to the interpretive questions. Visitors could engage the land/census comparison independently of the killing narrative. A modest website companion and educator sheets would extend access to the public exhibition; an online archive or school lesson alone is not the proposed NEH primary format. Exact venue, available floor area, objects and installation dates await institutional decisions and are not invented here. Planning would develop, rather than claim completion of, a final exhibition.

## Project evaluation

Prototype visitors would compare two sources, distinguish a directly stated fact from an inference, and identify a useful next record. The evaluation consultant would gather anonymous responses, observe confusing labels and record image-reading or access barriers. A proposed design target is that at least 75% of responding visitors can correctly distinguish the census reference date of 1 June 1860 from the enumeration date of 20 June 1860 after using the display. This is not a measured result. The evaluation report would include participant count, response count, question wording, anonymized totals, limitations and the revisions made.
